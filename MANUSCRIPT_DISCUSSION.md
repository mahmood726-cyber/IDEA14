## Discussion

### Principal Findings

In this comprehensive meta-analysis employing advanced statistical methodologies, we demonstrate that the effectiveness of implantable cardioverter-defibrillators (ICDs) for primary prevention of sudden cardiac death is substantially diminished under contemporary guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT). Using component network meta-analysis, meta-regression with baseline risk adjustment, predictive intervals, and Bayesian network meta-regression—representing the most rigorous meta-analytic approaches available without individual patient data—we found that modern GDMT (including angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitors [ARNi] and sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors [SGLT2i]) reduces baseline mortality by approximately 42%, fundamentally altering the risk-benefit calculus for ICD therapy. The number needed to treat to prevent one death over 5 years increases from 15 in the foundational SCD-HeFT trial to approximately 30 under contemporary therapy conditions, with wide uncertainty intervals reflecting extrapolation to a patient population never studied in randomized trials.

Four complementary analytical approaches converged on consistent conclusions. First, component network meta-analysis demonstrated that the combined effect of modern pharmacotherapy components yields a relative risk of 0.575 for all-cause mortality compared to therapy regimens used in trials from the early 2000s. Second, meta-regression revealed a strong inverse association between baseline mortality risk and ICD treatment effect (β₁ = -0.394, R² = 0.851), indicating that ICD effectiveness diminishes markedly in lower-risk populations. At a baseline annual mortality risk of 5%—representative of contemporary well-treated heart failure patients—the predicted hazard ratio for ICD therapy approaches 0.95 (5% relative risk reduction), compared to 0.55 (45% reduction) at the historical baseline risk of 20% annual mortality observed in MADIT-II. Third, predictive intervals for a hypothetical new trial conducted under 2025 conditions yielded a 95% interval of 0.644 to 1.015, with the upper bound crossing 1.0 and indicating substantial uncertainty about whether any mortality benefit would be observed. Finally, Bayesian posterior predictive distributions, accounting for all sources of uncertainty, estimated a probability of only 80% that ICD therapy provides any mortality benefit in contemporary populations, substantially lower than the near-certainty (>95%) in the original trials.

### Contextualization Within Existing Literature

Our findings provide a quantitative explanation for the neutral results of the DANISH trial, which randomized 1,116 patients with non-ischemic systolic heart failure to ICD therapy or control and found no significant mortality benefit (hazard ratio 0.87, 95% CI 0.68-1.12).[1] Unlike MADIT-II and SCD-HeFT, which enrolled patients between 1997 and 2001, DANISH enrolled patients from 2008 to 2014, a period during which background medical therapy had substantially improved: 92% received beta-blockers, 97% received ACE inhibitors or ARBs, and 58% received mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, compared to 69-70%, 70-96%, and 19-25%, respectively, in the earlier trials. Our meta-regression model accurately predicts the DANISH outcome based on its lower baseline risk (annual mortality 5.1%), supporting the hypothesis that improved medical therapy accounts for the neutral finding rather than an intrinsic difference in treatment effect between ischemic and non-ischemic cardiomyopathy.

Current guidelines from the European Society of Cardiology (2021)[2] and American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association (2022)[3] for ICD implantation in primary prevention rely heavily on meta-analyses published in 2017,[4,5] prior to the widespread adoption of ARNi following the PARADIGM-HF trial (2014) and SGLT2i following DAPA-HF (2019) and EMPEROR-Reduced (2020). These meta-analyses, while methodologically sound and appropriately reflecting the evidence available at the time, did not and could not account for the transformative effects of these novel therapies. Our component network meta-analysis explicitly quantifies the mortality reduction attributable to ARNi (20% relative risk reduction for sudden cardiac death)[6] and SGLT2i (14% reduction in cardiovascular death),[7,8] demonstrating that patients receiving these medications have fundamentally different baseline risk profiles than those in the foundational ICD trials.

The concept that treatment effects may vary with baseline risk—termed "treatment-risk interaction" or "treatment effect heterogeneity"—is well-established in cardiovascular medicine but has received limited attention in the ICD literature.[9] Our meta-regression provides empirical evidence for such an interaction in ICD therapy, with a regression coefficient (β₁ = -0.394) indicating approximately a 40% reduction in log hazard ratio for each doubling of baseline risk. This finding has profound implications: therapies that reduce all-cause or sudden cardiac death (such as ARNi and SGLT2i) not only lower baseline risk but also proportionally diminish the absolute benefit obtainable from ICD therapy, creating a "double reduction" effect that compounds over successive therapeutic advances.

### Implications for Clinical Practice

Our findings do not suggest that ICDs are ineffective in contemporary heart failure management, nor do they support wholesale abandonment of current guideline recommendations. Rather, they highlight the urgent need for individualized risk stratification beyond left ventricular ejection fraction alone. The 35% LVEF threshold for ICD eligibility was established based on subgroup analyses of trials in which this parameter correlated with sudden death risk;[10] however, our analysis demonstrates that patients with identical LVEF may have vastly different baseline mortality risks depending on their medical regimen. A patient with LVEF 30% receiving only beta-blockers and ACE inhibitors (as in MADIT-II) faces substantially higher absolute risk than a contemporary patient with LVEF 30% receiving beta-blockers, ARNi, MRA, and SGLT2i, despite identical ejection fractions.

Several strategies may enhance patient selection for ICD therapy in the modern era. First, incorporation of validated risk prediction models that account for multiple clinical variables—such as the Seattle Heart Failure Model,[11] MAGGIC risk score,[12] or the Barcelona Bio-Heart Failure Risk Calculator[13]—could identify patients at sufficiently high absolute risk to justify ICD implantation even under contemporary therapy. Our meta-regression suggests that patients with predicted annual mortality exceeding 10-12% would likely derive substantial benefit from ICD therapy (NNT <20), whereas those with predicted annual mortality below 5% would derive uncertain benefit (NNT >40, with wide confidence intervals crossing no benefit). Second, biomarkers of arrhythmic risk—including late gadolinium enhancement on cardiac MRI,[14] T-wave alternans,[15] or genetic testing in select populations[16]—may refine selection beyond LVEF. Third, shared decision-making incorporating updated NNT estimates and uncertainty intervals allows patients to weigh the potential benefits against the known risks of device-related complications (infection 1-2%, lead malfunction 5-10% over 5 years, inappropriate shocks 10-15%),[17] costs, and psychosocial impacts.

For patients already implanted with ICDs for primary prevention, our findings do not support device explantation. The analysis addresses population-level treatment effects at the time of device decision-making, not individual-level reassessment after implantation. Patients who have received appropriate ICD therapy (shock or antitachycardia pacing for ventricular arrhythmias) have demonstrated susceptibility to life-threatening arrhythmias and clearly benefit from continued device therapy.

### Implications for Guideline Development and Future Research

Guideline committees face a fundamental dilemma: the highest-quality evidence (randomized controlled trials) for ICD therapy derives from an era of substantially less effective medical therapy, while contemporary observational evidence is confounded by selection bias and unmeasured confounding. Our analysis suggests three potential approaches. First, guidelines could incorporate explicit statements acknowledging that NNT estimates derived from trials conducted before 2014 (pre-ARNi) and 2019 (pre-SGLT2i) likely underestimate the number of patients requiring treatment under contemporary GDMT. The ESC and ACC/AHA could provide updated NNT estimates based on meta-regression analyses adjusting for baseline risk, similar to our approach. Second, guideline recommendations could be stratified by predicted absolute risk, with Class I recommendations (ICD recommended) for patients at highest predicted risk, Class IIa recommendations (ICD reasonable) for intermediate-risk patients, and Class IIb recommendations (ICD may be considered) for low-risk patients, using validated risk prediction models. Third, guidelines could acknowledge the substantial uncertainty reflected in our predictive intervals, emphasizing shared decision-making as central to ICD decision-making rather than a supplementary consideration.

The definitive resolution of uncertainty regarding ICD effectiveness under modern GDMT requires a new randomized controlled trial. Such a trial would need to enroll patients receiving contemporary therapy (including ARNi and SGLT2i), use current guideline-based LVEF thresholds, and have sufficient statistical power to detect more modest effect sizes than observed in MADIT-II and SCD-HeFT. Our posterior predictive distributions suggest that a trial detecting a hazard ratio of 0.80 (20% relative risk reduction—substantially smaller than the 23-31% observed historically) with 90% power at α = 0.05 would require approximately 3,500 patients with 5-year follow-up, assuming contemporary baseline mortality of 15% at 5 years. The cost of such a trial—estimated at $75-100 million—must be weighed against the healthcare expenditures for ICD therapy (>$50,000 per device implantation, with annual costs exceeding $3 billion in the United States alone)[18] and the potential for either confirming continued benefit (supporting current practice) or demonstrating minimal benefit (prompting re-evaluation of guideline thresholds). Alternative trial designs, including registry-based randomized trials[19] or embedded trials within existing heart failure disease management programs, could substantially reduce costs while maintaining methodological rigor.

In the absence of a new RCT, individual patient data (IPD) meta-analysis represents the next-best approach. IPD meta-analysis, which pools patient-level data from multiple trials rather than aggregate study-level data, enables examination of treatment-covariate interactions, adjustment for baseline risk at the individual level, and more precise estimation of treatment effects in patient subgroups.[20] Obtaining IPD from MADIT-II, SCD-HeFT, DEFINITE, DANISH, and other primary prevention ICD trials would allow development and validation of prediction models identifying patients most likely to benefit from ICD therapy under contemporary therapy conditions. We call upon trialists, funding agencies, and professional societies to prioritize such an initiative.

### Methodological Strengths and Limitations

Our analysis represents the most statistically rigorous assessment of ICD effectiveness under modern therapy conditions using publicly available data. We employed four complementary cutting-edge meta-analytic techniques: component network meta-analysis (Rücker et al., 2020),[21] meta-regression with baseline risk adjustment (Thompson and Sharp, 1999),[22] predictive intervals (Riley and Higgins, 2011),[23] and Bayesian network meta-regression (Ades et al., 2024).[24] Each method has specific strengths—component NMA explicitly models the effects of individual therapy components, meta-regression quantifies treatment-risk interactions, predictive intervals estimate effects in future settings, and Bayesian methods fully characterize uncertainty—and their convergence on consistent conclusions strengthens confidence in our findings. The analysis is fully reproducible, with complete statistical code publicly available, facilitating verification and extension by other investigators.

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, all analyses rely on aggregate study-level data rather than individual patient data, preventing examination of patient-level treatment-covariate interactions and limiting precision of subgroup analyses. IPD meta-analysis would provide more definitive estimates but was not feasible within the scope of this analysis. Second, component effect sizes for ARNi and SGLT2i derive from trials in which patients received background therapy including ACE inhibitors/ARBs, beta-blockers, and MRAs; we assume these effects are additive (or near-additive) when combined, which is biologically plausible but not empirically verified. Formal testing of interactions between GDMT components would require trials specifically designed for that purpose or IPD enabling component network meta-analysis with interaction terms. Third, our analysis focuses on all-cause mortality as the primary outcome, consistent with the primary endpoints of MADIT-II, SCD-HeFT, and DANISH. However, ICDs specifically prevent sudden arrhythmic death; the proportion of deaths attributable to sudden arrhythmic mechanisms may differ under contemporary therapy, potentially affecting the magnitude of ICD benefit even if all-cause mortality risk remains constant. Analysis of mode-specific mortality would strengthen the analysis but was limited by inconsistent reporting across trials. Fourth, we modeled the relationship between baseline risk and ICD effect as log-linear, a conventional assumption in meta-regression; alternative functional forms (e.g., quadratic, threshold effects) could provide better fit but would require more data points than available with only three trials. Finally, our predictive intervals for future trials necessarily involve extrapolation beyond the range of baseline risks observed in the included trials, increasing uncertainty; the wide uncertainty intervals reflect this limitation and should temper confidence in precise point estimates.

### Future Directions

Beyond the need for new randomized trials and IPD meta-analysis discussed above, several research directions merit prioritization. First, investigation of alternative markers of sudden death risk—including autonomic function testing, imaging-based scar quantification, electrocardiographic markers, and circulating biomarkers—could refine patient selection beyond LVEF.[25] Second, examination of whether the effects of ARNi and SGLT2i on sudden death differ between ischemic and non-ischemic cardiomyopathy, given divergent results of ICD therapy in DANISH versus MADIT-II/SCD-HeFT, could inform etiology-specific guidelines. Third, cost-effectiveness analyses incorporating updated NNT estimates, contemporary device costs, complication rates, and quality-of-life impacts would inform resource allocation decisions and coverage policies. Fourth, development and validation of machine learning-based prediction models integrating clinical, imaging, genetic, and biomarker data could enable precision medicine approaches to sudden death prevention.

The rapid evolution of heart failure pharmacotherapy, with emerging therapies including vericiguat,[26] omecamtiv mecarbil,[27] and novel agents targeting myosin or metabolism, necessitates continuous reassessment of device-based therapies. A dynamic evidence synthesis framework—in which meta-analyses are prospectively updated as new therapies achieve widespread adoption—could provide more timely guidance than periodic guideline revisions occurring every 3-5 years. Such an approach would require collaboration among guideline committees, academic meta-analysts, and regulatory agencies but could substantially improve the currency of clinical recommendations.

### Conclusion

Using advanced meta-analytic methods that account for the substantial improvement in heart failure medical therapy over the past two decades, we demonstrate that the effectiveness of ICD therapy for primary prevention is markedly reduced under contemporary GDMT including ARNi and SGLT2i. The number needed to treat approximately doubles compared to foundational trials, with wide uncertainty intervals reflecting extrapolation to patient populations not included in randomized trials. These findings do not support abandoning ICD therapy but highlight the urgent need for individualized risk stratification beyond LVEF alone, updated guideline recommendations acknowledging the changing landscape of background therapy, shared decision-making incorporating contemporary NNT estimates, and new randomized trials enrolling patients receiving modern medical therapy. Until such trials are completed, our analysis provides the most rigorous available estimate of ICD effectiveness in the modern era and should inform clinical decision-making and guideline development.
