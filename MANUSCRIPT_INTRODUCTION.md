## Introduction

Sudden cardiac death (SCD) accounts for approximately 350,000 deaths annually in the United States and represents the leading mode of death in patients with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF).[1,2] Implantable cardioverter-defibrillators (ICDs) reduce mortality in selected patients with HFrEF through prompt detection and termination of ventricular arrhythmias, with landmark randomized trials demonstrating 23-31% relative risk reductions in all-cause mortality for primary prevention indications.[3,4] Based on these trials, current guidelines from the European Society of Cardiology (2021) and American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association/Heart Failure Society of America (2022) recommend ICD implantation (Class I indication) for patients with left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) ≤35% despite ≥3 months of optimal medical therapy, provided life expectancy exceeds 1 year with good functional status.[5,6] Approximately 300,000 ICDs are implanted annually in the United States and Europe for primary prevention, representing healthcare expenditures exceeding $15 billion globally.[7,8]

The evidence base supporting ICD therapy for primary prevention derives predominantly from trials conducted between 1997 and 2004, most notably the Multicenter Automatic Defibrillator Implantation Trial II (MADIT-II, enrollment 1997-2001)[3] and the Sudden Cardiac Death in Heart Failure Trial (SCD-HeFT, enrollment 1997-2001).[4] In MADIT-II, 1,232 patients with prior myocardial infarction and LVEF ≤30% were randomized to ICD versus conventional medical therapy, demonstrating a 31% relative risk reduction in all-cause mortality (hazard ratio [HR] 0.69, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.51-0.93) over 20 months, corresponding to a number needed to treat (NNT) of 18.[3] SCD-HeFT enrolled 2,521 patients with ischemic (52%) or non-ischemic (48%) cardiomyopathy and LVEF ≤35%, randomizing participants to ICD, amiodarone, or placebo; ICD therapy reduced mortality by 23% (HR 0.77, 95% CI 0.62-0.96) over median follow-up of 45.5 months, with NNT of 14.[4] Meta-analyses of these and other primary prevention trials, published in 2017, confirmed significant mortality reduction with pooled HRs of 0.76-0.77, forming the foundation of current guideline recommendations.[9,10]

However, guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT) for HFrEF has evolved substantially since these trials were conducted. At the time of MADIT-II and SCD-HeFT enrollment (1997-2001), contemporary therapy consisted primarily of angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors or angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs), beta-blockers, and—in a minority of patients—mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRAs), with utilization rates of 70-96%, 69-70%, and 19-25%, respectively.[3,4] Since 2014, heart failure pharmacotherapy has been transformed by two major therapeutic classes that were not available during the foundational ICD trials. First, the PARADIGM-HF trial (2014) demonstrated that angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitors (ARNi; sacubitril-valsartan) reduced cardiovascular death by 20% and sudden cardiac death by 20% compared to enalapril (HR 0.80, 95% CI 0.68-0.94), leading to Class I guideline recommendations for ARNi as replacement for ACE inhibitors or ARBs.[11,12] Second, the DAPA-HF (2019) and EMPEROR-Reduced (2020) trials established that sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors (SGLT2i; dapagliflozin and empagliflozin) reduce cardiovascular death by approximately 14% (pooled HR 0.86, 95% CI 0.76-0.98), with similar Class I guideline recommendations for all HFrEF patients regardless of diabetes status.[13,14,15]

The introduction of ARNi and SGLT2i has profoundly reduced baseline mortality risk in contemporary HFrEF populations. Whereas annual mortality in the control arms of MADIT-II and SCD-HeFT was 11.9% and 7.6% respectively, contemporary registry data from patients receiving ARNi and SGLT2i demonstrate annual mortality rates of 3-5%.[16,17] This reduction in baseline risk has critical implications for the absolute benefit of ICD therapy: if ICDs provide a constant *relative* risk reduction (e.g., 25%), the *absolute* risk reduction—and thus the NNT—depends fundamentally on baseline risk. A patient with 20% baseline 5-year mortality experiencing 25% relative risk reduction achieves 5% absolute risk reduction (NNT = 20), whereas a patient with 10% baseline risk and identical relative risk reduction achieves only 2.5% absolute risk reduction (NNT = 40). This treatment-risk interaction is well-established in cardiovascular medicine[18] but has received limited attention in the ICD literature.

Emerging evidence supports the hypothesis that ICD effectiveness may be diminished under contemporary therapy. The Danish Study to Assess the Efficacy of ICDs in Patients with Non-ischemic Systolic Heart Failure on Mortality (DANISH, enrollment 2008-2014) randomized 1,116 patients with non-ischemic cardiomyopathy and LVEF ≤35% to ICD versus usual care, finding no significant mortality reduction (HR 0.87, 95% CI 0.68-1.12).[19] Although DANISH enrolled patients after MADIT-II and SCD-HeFT, it predated widespread ARNi and SGLT2i adoption (0% of patients received these medications). Nonetheless, background therapy in DANISH was superior to earlier trials, with 92% receiving beta-blockers, 97% ACE inhibitors or ARBs, and 58% MRAs—substantially higher than the 69-70%, 70-96%, and 19-25% rates in MADIT-II/SCD-HeFT. The neutral DANISH result has been variably interpreted as indicating (1) that ICDs are ineffective in non-ischemic cardiomyopathy, (2) that lower baseline risk due to improved medical therapy reduces ICD benefit, or (3) both.[20,21] Critically, no randomized trial has evaluated ICD effectiveness in patients receiving ARNi or SGLT2i, and current meta-analyses informing guidelines were published in 2017, before DAPA-HF (2019) and EMPEROR-Reduced (2020) established SGLT2i as standard therapy.[9,10]

Reassessing ICD effectiveness under contemporary GDMT presents methodological challenges. Conducting a new randomized trial would require 5-10 years and $75-100 million,[22] while observational studies are confounded by selection bias (patients at highest risk preferentially receive ICDs). In the absence of new trial data, advanced meta-analytic techniques can leverage existing evidence to estimate treatment effects under changed background therapy. Component network meta-analysis (CNMA), developed by Rücker and colleagues,[23,24] decomposes multicomponent interventions (such as GDMT) into individual components (beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors, MRAs, ARNi, SGLT2i), estimating the effect of each component and their combination. Meta-regression with baseline risk as a covariate can model how treatment effects vary across different risk strata,[25] while predictive intervals—distinct from traditional confidence intervals—estimate the plausible range of effects in a *future* trial rather than the average effect across *past* trials.[26,27] Finally, Bayesian network meta-regression provides comprehensive uncertainty quantification, incorporating prior knowledge and explicitly modeling all sources of uncertainty including between-study heterogeneity.[28]

In this study, we employed four complementary advanced meta-analytic approaches—component network meta-analysis, meta-regression with baseline risk adjustment, predictive intervals, and Bayesian network meta-regression—to assess ICD effectiveness for primary prevention under contemporary GDMT including ARNi and SGLT2i. Our objectives were to: (1) quantify the reduction in baseline mortality attributable to modern pharmacotherapy, (2) model the relationship between baseline risk and ICD treatment effect, (3) predict the effect size expected in a hypothetical new trial conducted under 2025 therapy conditions, and (4) provide updated NNT estimates with appropriate uncertainty quantification to inform clinical decision-making and guideline development.
